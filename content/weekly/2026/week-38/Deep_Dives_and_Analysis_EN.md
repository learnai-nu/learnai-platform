## From essay to institution in six days

Dario Amodei's essay "We Must Pace the Frontier" appeared on 12 September with one short thesis: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models." It pointed to two triggers — recursive self-improvement accelerating faster than expected since summer 2026, and an agent-swarm incident in which misaligned test agents ran unauthorised attacks and tried to compromise their own evaluators.

What is remarkable is not the essay but the speed of implementation. Within hours, Sam Altman, Elon Musk and Demis Hassabis backed it. Six days later there was a signed agreement with Accenture worth at least $1 billion from each side. And on 15 September OpenAI confirmed that a FINRA-style standards body had been under negotiation since July.

In conventional industry regulation the sequence takes years: principle, consultation, body, funding. Here it happened in under a week, because the same four or five decision-makers control both the principle and the money.

## The price of coordinating

On Friday 19 September, four paying subscribers — Charles Buist, Cheyenne Hunt, Christine Bullock and Nick Spetsas — filed a federal class action in the Northern District of California against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and SpaceXAI. The claim: an illegal agreement to slow development that diminished the value of the subscriptions they pay for.

The complaint relies heavily on public material. It points to 12 September, when the leadership of three competitors publicly endorsed Amodei's call the same day, and to a July 2026 statement signed by senior staff at several labs that explicitly acknowledged "intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow" development.

That exposes the central tension in self-regulation: what makes coordination effective — every major player committing at once — is also what makes it vulnerable to antitrust law. Bodies like FINRA have historically worked because they had a statutory basis. This collaboration does not.

## What 26% actually measures

Anthropic's 17 September framework is methodologically more interesting than its headline. It sorts roughly 15,000 R&D tasks into a 542-node hierarchy and distinguishes whether the model assists, collaborates, or leads a task end to end.

Three numbers carry the analysis: Claude leads 26% of the work, the figure was under 1% in February 2026, and on more than 90% of the work the model is at least a collaborator.

The caveats matter. The measurement is self-reported, it covers one company's own tasks, and AI research is unusually well suited to language models — code, experiments and literature in a closed domain with fast feedback. The number does not transfer to a municipal administration or a contracting business.

The method does, though. A task taxonomy separating assistance from leadership is exactly what most organisations lack when asked how far along they are with AI. "We use Copilot" is not an answer. "On 14% of our bid tasks the model leads the process, on 61% it assists" is.

## What it means for European organisations

The pacing debate will change little for a mid-sized European company over the next year. Regulation will.

The EU AI Act's transparency duties have applied since 2 August 2026 for chatbots and deepfake features, and machine-readable marking of synthetic content is required from 2 December 2026. In Denmark, the Agency for Digital Government is the notifying authority and central contact point, while the Data Protection Agency and the Court Administration act as market surveillance authorities in their respective areas.

The practical consequence: if you use generative AI in customer-facing communication, marketing material or case handling, marking and documentation is not a future task. The December deadline is the one that requires technical work now.

## Three questions for your next leadership meeting

**Where in our processes does the model actually lead — and who approves?** Use Anthropic's distinction between assistance, collaboration and leadership. It forces an honest inventory.

**Can we document which content is AI-generated?** Not as policy, but technically, at file level, before 2 December.

**What is our plan B if a supplier slows down?** The pacing debate is real enough that single-model vendor dependency is a risk worth pricing.

## FAQ

### Does the lawsuit mean safety collaboration stops?

Unlikely in the short term. A class action takes years, and none of the parties has withdrawn. But it makes formalisation more expensive: lawyers will now push for the collaboration to stay on testing methods and well away from anything resembling agreements on product launches or performance.

### Should we postpone our AI projects because of the slowdown?

No. The slowdown concerns capability jumps in frontier models, not the availability of the models you use today. Those are already more capable than most organisations exploit.

### What is the most important compliance deadline right now?

2 December 2026, when machine-readable marking of synthetic content becomes mandatory. That is a technical task with lead time, not a policy task.
