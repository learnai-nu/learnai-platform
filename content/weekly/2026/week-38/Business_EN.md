**Standfirst:** Chip stocks fell on Monday's pacing debate, Anthropic moved its listing to November and OpenAI ruled out 2026 — all while capital expenditure plans stayed unchanged.

## Monday's selloff

Wall Street closed down on Monday 14 September, dragged by losses in Nvidia and other chipmakers after senior executives at US AI companies warned about risks from rapid development and called for a slowdown.

The numbers: Nvidia fell 3.4%. The PHLX semiconductor index lost almost 6% — its worst day since early July. But the Nasdaq Composite ended only 0.56% down, because a number of big tech and software stocks rose.

That spread is the story. The market punished the companies selling shovels and rewarded the ones selling software. If capability jumps slow, demand for next-generation training clusters falls first — while the models that already exist still have to be sold, integrated and operated.

## Two listings, two opposite signals

Anthropic has moved its planned IPO from October to November. The delay lets the company present fresh third-quarter numbers before pricing is locked. Investors are discussing a valuation around $2 trillion and a raise of up to $100 billion, with Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs involved.

The revenue behind those numbers: an annualised run rate above $65 billion at the end of July 2026, up from roughly $47 billion in May and about $9 billion at the end of 2025. Investors cited by the Wall Street Journal expect more than $110 billion by the end of 2026. At $2 trillion that is roughly 31 times current annualised revenue.

In the opposite direction, Sam Altman said in remarks published on Saturday that OpenAI will not go public in 2026 as previously expected: "given everything happening with safety, right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public."

Two companies, the same week, the same safety debate — and opposite conclusions about the capital markets. That is a useful signal of how large the uncertainty actually is.

## The lawsuit as procurement risk

On Friday 19 September, four paying subscribers filed a federal class action in the Northern District of California against Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and SpaceXAI. The claim is that the companies made an illegal agreement to slow development, diminishing the value of the subscriptions the plaintiffs pay for.

For a European buyer, the legal outcome matters less than what the case reveals about supplier roadmap risk. If coordinating on testing requirements becomes expensive to defend, suppliers may become more reticent about announcing concrete launch plans — which makes vendor management harder, not easier.

Practical consequence: write exit and model-switching clauses into new agreements. Do not assume the model you build on today will have the same capability curve in 18 months.

## Infrastructure is not slowing

While the debate ran, the capital commitments continued. Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta are together expected to spend about $600 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026. Meta alone guides $115–145 billion in capex, most of it earmarked for AI infrastructure.

The other side shows up in headcount: a large share of the most visible 2026 tech layoffs are at companies simultaneously pouring money into AI infrastructure. Oracle's headcount fell by 21,000 over the year, with the savings feeding a data centre buildout.

In week 38, Intel spinoff Cornelis Networks raised $205 million and introduced Active Compute Fabric, an open, GPU-agnostic networking layer for AI clusters. And Australian AI data-centre company Firmus is seeking to raise as much as A$7 billion — about US$5 billion — in an ASX listing targeted for the end of October.

## The management arithmetic

While capital markets debate pace, the real bottleneck inside companies is still execution. Recent surveys indicate only 31% of organisations have an agent running in production, and that 29% see significant returns from generative AI against 23% from agents. Median time-to-value on agent deployments is around 5.1 months.

At the same time, 66% report productivity and efficiency as the primary benefit, while 74% hope for revenue growth and only 20% already see it.

That is the number that should drive 2027 budgeting: the gain is real, but it is a cost gain, not a growth gain — until the processes around the model are rebuilt. Set return expectations on a 5–9 month horizon rather than the quarter, and pick processes where you own the data and the approval flow.

## FAQ

### Is the selloff a signal to cut AI investment?

No. The fall hit semiconductor stocks primarily, meaning expectations for next-generation training capacity. It says nothing about the value of applying the models that already exist. The companies' own capex plans were not changed during the week.

### Should we wait on larger AI contracts until the lawsuit is resolved?

No, but negotiate differently. The case may take years. Use it instead as an argument for shorter lock-in periods, explicit exit clauses, and the right to switch model provider without rebuilding the integration.

### Which internal metric should we track?

The share of processes where an AI-supported workflow is in production — not in pilot. The benchmark is around 31%, and median time to value is about five months. If you have piloted longer than that without production, the problem is organisational, not technical.
