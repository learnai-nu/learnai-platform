# When the frontier model became free in the classroom

**Standfirst:** OpenAI shipped a college planner for teenagers on 7 October and gave every free user GPT-6 the next day. Access is no longer the barrier — assessment is.

## What OpenAI shipped

On 7 October, OpenAI launched College Planner in ChatGPT for Teens — a tool meant to help young people with applications to higher education and with working out how to pay for them. It arrived alongside flashcards, quizzes and the formation of a teen AI council.

OpenAI also backed College Advising Corps, a nonprofit placing recent graduates as advisers in under-resourced high schools. The contribution is intended to help the organisation expand into new states and establish an AI fellowship programme.

It is a US launch addressing a US problem — access to guidance counselling is far more unevenly distributed there than in most of Europe. But the product logic is the same everywhere: the model moves from answering questions to structuring an extended process with milestones and deadlines.

The same week, OpenAI Academy ran "AI Skills Jam" office hours for K-12 educators and a Codex bootcamp on 7 October.

## The free tier is the real story

On 8 October, GPT-6 reached Free and Go users, after paid tiers had received access the day before.

For education that is a bigger event than any of the product launches. Any assumption that students reach weaker models than the institution does is void. That applies not only to school students — it applies to adult learners on a professional course and to staff in a company's internal training.

The concrete consequence: assignment formats considered robust because they required a good model no longer are. Work that a frontier model can complete without a trace can now be completed by anyone.

This is not an argument for banning anything. It is an argument for moving assessment to where the process is visible — oral defence, work produced under supervision, or tasks where the student must account for what the model contributed and where it was wrong.

## The price of an education tool fell the same week

On 7 October, Anthropic priced Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 input and $0.50 output per million tokens for prompts under 100,000 tokens — roughly 75% cheaper on average than its predecessor.

For anyone building learning tools that moves a boundary. Individual feedback on every submission, automatically generated practice tasks adapted to each learner, or a conversation partner for every participant on a thousand-person course — that has until now cost enough to require a business justification. It largely does not any more.

The constraint therefore moves from the budget to the pedagogy: not "can we afford to give everyone feedback", but "what kind of feedback actually makes someone learn something".

## What it means for workplace learning

For an organisation running internal capability building, the week has three practical consequences.

**Access is no longer a design parameter.** Do not build a programme whose value consists of giving participants access to a good tool. That value is now free.

**The spread in outcomes widens.** When everyone has the same model, the difference in what they get out of it is set by how well each person can frame, judge and correct. That is the capability a programme should train — not operation.

**Assessment must separate process from product.** A finished document now says very little about what the participant can do. Have them show the work.

That is also the best reason to keep investing in AI ambassadors and peer knowledge-sharing rather than pure tool rollout: what needs spreading is judgement, and judgement spreads through people.

## The unanswered question

None of the week's launches answers whether these tools actually improve learning. It remains an area with strikingly little high-quality research, and the studies that exist point in different directions depending on age group and subject.

Until that knowledge exists, the honest position is to treat AI in teaching as a well-founded experiment with measurement points — not as a settled improvement.

## FAQ

### Should we ban AI in our internal programmes?

No, and it could not be enforced now that a frontier model is free. Move assessment instead to something that shows the process: an oral presentation, work under supervision, or an account of what the model contributed and where it was wrong.

### What should an AI course contain then?

Judgement rather than operation. How to frame a task, how to check an answer, when not to use the model, and what to do with data that must not leave the building. People learn the operation themselves in an afternoon.

### Is there evidence this works?

Not strong evidence, no. The field lacks high-quality research, and results vary by age group and subject. Treat the effort as an experiment with measurement points, and be honest about that internally.
