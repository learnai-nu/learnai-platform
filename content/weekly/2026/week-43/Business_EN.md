## Chips as an alternative to the incumbents

On 19 October Tenstorrent raised $1.5 billion in a Series E at a $6.2 billion valuation, led by Trace Capital and Innovation X. The company develops AI processors, server systems and licensable chip technology based on the open RISC-V architecture.

The argument is control: customers, especially large enterprises and sovereign projects, want alternatives to the closed ecosystems that dominate AI computing today.

## A billion for an idea

Flapping Airplanes raised a $1 billion Series A at a $6 billion valuation, co-led by Index Ventures and Lux Capital with Sequoia and GV among others. According to Bloomberg it has neither a commercial product nor revenue. It is working to reduce the compute and data needed to train and run AI models.

This is investment in fundamental research: investors bet that breakthroughs in efficiency will be very valuable in an industry increasingly constrained by power and hardware.

## A billion for customers who already pay

The same week Legora, which builds AI for lawyers, raised $550 million at an $8.8 billion valuation. Here the rationale is the opposite: according to coverage it has more than $200 million in annual recurring revenue and broad adoption among major law firms.

The contrast is instructive. Both companies are worth billions, one because investors believe in a research breakthrough, the other because customers already pay. Specialised software for one industry again shows there is money in solving a specific problem well.

## Salesforce assembles its marketing suite

On 20 October Salesforce agreed to acquire Braze for about $5.66 billion, or $47.50 in cash per share. Braze provides real-time engagement to around 2,770 customers and will strengthen Salesforce's Marketing Cloud. The deal is expected to close in Salesforce's fiscal year 2028.

Together with the Loops acquisition in week 42 a picture emerges: the large platforms are buying the parts that let their systems act on their own, not just show data.

## What it means for you

**Follow the infrastructure.** Investment in chips and efficiency is one reason AI keeps getting cheaper. That argues for recalculating your business cases regularly.

**Look for specialised software in your own industry.** Legora shows strong specialist tools are arriving. Check what exists for your field.

**Review contracts when vendors are acquired.** When suppliers like Braze change owner, terms and product direction can change.

## FAQ

### Why invest a billion in a company with no revenue?

Because investors judge that a breakthrough in AI efficiency could be very valuable. It is a high-risk bet, not a sign the company makes money.

### Will these investments make AI cheaper?

Probably over time. More chip alternatives and more efficient models push down the price per task.

### Should we choose specialised AI over general tools?

Not either-or. General tools cover broadly, but in demanding fields such as law, specialist tools can add more value. Test both on your own tasks.
